A painting has one frame.

A film has thousands.

The strange achievement of painterly cinema is making those thousands feel composed rather than captured.

## The rectangle comes first

Before there is movement, there is placement.

A face against a doorway. A body at the edge of a landscape. A strip of red surrounded by green. Two figures separated by an impossible amount of empty room.

This is where cinema and painting share their oldest problem: deciding what belongs inside the rectangle.

The difference is that a filmmaker has to solve it again every time somebody moves.

## Barry Lyndon borrowed the eighteenth century

Stanley Kubrick did not simply make *Barry Lyndon* look old. He researched the visual culture of the period and built the film around it.

Hogarth, Gainsborough, Constable and other painters informed its interiors, landscapes, costumes and compositions. Kubrick and cinematographer John Alcott then pushed the idea further by photographing interiors with natural and historically plausible sources, including scenes illuminated largely by candlelight.

The result is not a film with paintings in it.

The film behaves like one.

People sit rigidly inside enormous rooms. Landscapes dwarf their occupants. Candlelight separates faces from darkness. Even seduction can resemble a tableau in which almost nothing physically moves.

The beauty creates distance rather than intimacy.

Everyone has been perfectly placed.

Everyone looks trapped.

## Days of Heaven paints with available light

Terrence Malick and cinematographer Néstor Almendros arrived at the same destination from the opposite direction.

Instead of controlling every source, they stripped much of the artificial lighting away.

The wheat fields of *Days of Heaven* are photographed in natural light, often when the sun is low enough to flatten the landscape into silhouettes, gold and enormous skies.

Vermeer, Edward Hopper and Andrew Wyeth were among the painters informing the film's visual language.

That matters because the landscape is not background.

It is frequently larger than the people supposedly occupying the story.

Malick does not photograph characters standing in beautiful scenery.

He makes them temporary marks inside it.

## In the Mood for Love puts emotion on the walls

Wong Kar-wai does something more claustrophobic.

*In the Mood for Love* is filled with narrow corridors, doorframes, mirrors, patterned wallpaper, red curtains and saturated pockets of light.

Chow and Su rarely seem to possess the whole frame.

Something is always interrupting it.

A wall.

A doorway.

Another person.

The edge of a mirror.

Christopher Doyle and Mark Lee Ping-bin's cinematography turns obstruction into emotional grammar. The characters cannot openly express what exists between them, so the environment appears to do it instead.

Red becomes more intense than their dialogue. Rooms feel smaller than their desire. Maggie Cheung's changing cheongsams can even register the passage of time before the narrative announces it.

The characters remain controlled.

The image does not.

## Ran uses people as pigment

Akira Kurosawa began as a painter.

In *Ran*, that history becomes difficult to separate from his filmmaking.

Armies do not merely identify opposing sides. Red, yellow and blue become compositional units moving across enormous green landscapes.

From a distance, individual soldiers almost disappear.

What remains is color.

A yellow mass crosses the field. Red enters from another direction. Black smoke destroys the clarity of the image.

The battle can be read before every individual action can be understood.

That is the painter's instinct operating at cinematic scale: color is not applied after composition.

Color *is* composition.

## Blade Runner 2049 makes the figure small

Roger Deakins and Denis Villeneuve repeatedly place human beings against structures and landscapes too large for them.

K walks through orange ruins.

He stands beneath advertisements several stories high.

Figures become silhouettes against fog, concrete and artificial light.

The technique belongs to a long tradition of landscape painting: reduce the human figure and the environment suddenly acquires psychological weight.

Scale tells the story before dialogue has to.

The person is small.

The world is not.

## The Fall makes reality irrelevant

Then there are films that abandon naturalism almost completely.

Tarsem Singh's *The Fall* treats locations, costumes and bodies as graphic material.

A staircase becomes geometry.

A desert becomes negative space.

A red costume becomes the center of an otherwise restrained composition.

The question is no longer whether the image looks realistic.

It is whether the image communicates.

This is where painterly cinema separates itself from beautiful cinematography.

Beauty is an effect.

Composition is a decision.

## The test

Pause the film.

Then remove the actors from your imagination.

If the remaining architecture, light, color and empty space are still telling you something, the frame was doing more than recording a performance.

Now press play.

Everything moves.

And the painting has to be made again.
