# The Space Between Things

Studio Ghibli understands something modern cinema often forgets: silence is not the absence of storytelling.

Hayao Miyazaki’s films are filled with moments where almost nothing happens. A girl waits for a bus in the rain. A train crosses a flooded landscape. Someone lies in the grass listening to the radio. A pilot quietly repairs his plane beside the sea.

The story seems to stop.

But emotionally, it doesn't.

These pauses are connected to the Japanese concept of **Ma (間)** — the space between things.

And they may be the secret behind why Studio Ghibli films feel so alive.

## 01 — The Space Between the Claps

In a well-known interview, Miyazaki demonstrated Ma by clapping his hands several times and drawing attention to the silence between each clap.

The important thing isn't only the clap.

It's the interval that allows one clap to become distinct from the next.

Cinema works the same way.

Modern commercial filmmaking often fears empty time. Dialogue overlaps dialogue. Jokes arrive before the previous joke has settled. Music tells us what to feel. Cuts constantly redirect our attention.

The assumption is simple:

If nothing is happening, the audience might become bored.

Miyazaki works from almost the opposite assumption.

Sometimes nothing needs to happen.

A character can simply sit.

The wind can move through grass.

Rain can hit the pavement.

A train can continue toward somewhere we cannot yet see.

These moments aren't interruptions.

They are part of the rhythm.

---

## 02 — Nothing Happens

Ma is often translated as **negative space**, **interval**, **emptiness**, or **pause**, but none of those translations completely capture it.

The concept appears throughout Japanese aesthetics.

In architecture, the empty space helps define the objects surrounding it.

In music, silence gives individual notes rhythm and weight.

In visual art, an unoccupied area can be as important as the subject itself.

Cinema has its own version.

A filmmaker can deliberately remove dialogue, plot information, music, or action and allow time itself to occupy the frame.

But Ma shouldn't simply be confused with slowness.

A long scene isn't automatically meaningful because it is long.

What matters is the space the filmmaker creates for the audience.

And Studio Ghibli repeatedly uses that space to let emotions exist without immediately explaining them.

In many films, emptiness is something an editor removes.

**In Ghibli, emptiness isn't an interruption of the story. It is part of the story.**

---

## 03 — The Train

One of the clearest examples appears in *Spirited Away*.

Chihiro boards a train with No-Face and travels across a landscape submerged beneath water.

For several minutes, remarkably little happens.

There is no major confrontation.

No exposition.

No joke every few seconds.

No villain interrupts the journey.

Chihiro simply sits beside the window while the train moves forward.

Outside, telephone poles rise from the water. Small islands pass in the distance. Shadow-like passengers enter and leave.

The film allows the journey to remain a journey.

Yet psychologically, an enormous amount is happening.

Chihiro has already survived fear, separation, work, danger, and transformation. She is moving toward something new without knowing exactly what waits for her.

She has left one emotional state but hasn't reached another.

The physical journey becomes an emotional interval.

Miyazaki doesn't need Chihiro to explain any of this.

He gives us enough silence to feel it ourselves.

That is Ma functioning as narrative.

## 04 — Waiting for the Bus

*My Neighbor Totoro* uses the same principle differently.

Satsuki and Mei stand at a rural bus stop at night.

It is raining.

They wait.

And wait.

Water falls from leaves. Puddles collect beneath their feet. The darkness surrounding them feels enormous.

<figure>
  <img src="/media/journal/the-space-between-things/waiting-for-the-bus.png" alt="Two small figures waiting at a rural bus stop at night in the rain, wet pavement reflecting a single lamp, tall dark trees behind them" />
  <figcaption>Before anything magical arrives, we get the rain.</figcaption>
</figure>

Then Totoro appears.

Again, the scene doesn't rush toward the fantastic event.

Miyazaki spends time establishing something completely ordinary: the physical experience of waiting for transportation in bad weather.

That ordinariness matters.

When a giant forest spirit eventually stands beside the children, the impossible somehow feels believable.

Ghibli doesn't make fantasy feel real by endlessly explaining its worlds.

**It surrounds fantasy with ordinary life.**

Rain.

Mud.

Food.

Work.

Waiting.

Sleeping.

Walking.

These mundane experiences become anchors. The magical elements can become extraordinary because everything surrounding them feels tangible.

## 05 — The Texture of Being Alive

This pattern appears throughout Miyazaki's work.

<figure>
  <img src="/media/journal/the-space-between-things/texture-of-being-alive.png" alt="A person lying in tall grass seen from ground level, a small radio beside them, a vast sky of slow-moving clouds overhead" />
  <figcaption>The plot is not doing anything right now. Neither is she. That's fine.</figcaption>
</figure>

In *Kiki's Delivery Service*, Kiki lies in the grass listening to the radio while clouds move overhead.

In *Porco Rosso*, Porco spends time alone in his hidden cove, surrounded by sea, rock, machinery, and silence.

Elsewhere, characters cook meals, clean rooms, repair machines, stare through windows, travel somewhere, or simply watch the weather change.

These scenes rarely exist to deliver information.

They establish texture.

They remind us that characters continue existing even when the plot isn't demanding something from them.

Real life works this way too.

Most of our lives aren't climaxes.

They are the moments between them.

Waiting.

Eating.

Walking.

Repairing.

Looking through a window.

Watching clouds.

Listening to rain.

Ghibli treats these experiences as worthy of animation.

They aren't filler scenes between important events.

**They're what make the events important.**

---

## 06 — You Need Silence to Hear the Explosion

There is also a structural reason Ma works.

Emotion depends on contrast.

A movie operating continuously at emotional volume ten eventually makes ten feel like five.

If everything is spectacular, spectacle becomes ordinary.

If everything is dramatic, drama becomes noise.

Quiet restores the scale.

Ghibli frequently creates a rhythm closer to:

**Stillness → anticipation → event → stillness**

rather than:

**Event → event → event → event**

The pause changes how we experience what comes next.

A storm feels stronger after watching calm water.

Fear becomes sharper after safety.

Wonder becomes more powerful after boredom.

Even silence itself begins carrying tension.

This is why Ma shouldn't be understood as simply slowing a movie down.

It is part of the film's emotional architecture.

The absence creates contrast.

And contrast gives events weight.

---

## 07 — The Audience Completes the Scene

There is another reason these moments feel unusually personal.

Miyazaki often refuses to tell us exactly what a character is thinking.

Someone looks through a window.

Someone watches rain.

Someone sits quietly after something difficult has happened.

Many films would insert dialogue.

Or narration.

Or music designed to clearly identify the emotion.

Ghibli often leaves the space open.

And something interesting happens.

We fill it.

A quiet train ride might remind one viewer of loneliness and another of leaving home.

Rain against a window might evoke childhood for one person and grief for someone else.

The filmmaker provides the image.

The audience supplies part of the emotion.

Ma therefore creates something that exposition cannot.

Participation.

The viewer isn't simply receiving meaning.

They are completing it.

---

## The Editorial Verdict

Cinema is usually discussed in terms of what filmmakers put inside the frame:

Characters.

Dialogue.

Movement.

Music.

Spectacle.

Miyazaki asks us to notice what he leaves out.

The unanswered sentence.

The empty landscape.

The train ride.

The rain before the bus arrives.

The few seconds when nobody needs to say anything.

Ma gives those absences shape.

And perhaps that is why Ghibli memories often feel strangely like our own.

We don't remember life only through its great events.

We remember looking through windows.

Waiting for someone.

Hearing rain against a roof.

Watching afternoon light move across a room.

The space between things is not empty.

**It is where experience accumulates.**
